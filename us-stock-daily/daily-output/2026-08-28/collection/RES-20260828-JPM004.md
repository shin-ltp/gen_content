Building resilience by expecting, preparing for and welcoming change is important for the longevity of generative families.

A series with Dennis T. Jaffe, Ph.D., in partnership with J.P. Morgan Wealth Management’s Family Engagement and Governance team.

Dr. Jaffe, a Senior Research Fellow at BanyanGlobal Family Wealth Advisors, is a leader and author in the field of family enterprise consulting. An organizational consultant and a clinical psychologist, he helps multigenerational families develop governance practices that build the capability of next-generation leadership and advises financial organizations and family offices on serving family clients.

J.P. Morgan is honored to share a special series highlighting Dr. Jaffe’s findings from his 2021 book, “Borrowed From Your Grandchildren: The Evolution of 100-Year Family Enterprises.”

By the third generation of a family enterprise, none of the families in Dr. Jaffe’s study on generative families contained the same business mix with which they began. In this insight, we take a closer look at the process of evolution that family businesses undergo. In his study, Dr. Jaffe found that each family generation interviewed redefined and renewed its business and financial activity to sustain their wealth for a growing number of family members. Previously effective business practices need to be updated and redefined for new realities. The family, not designed to allow huge changes, must learn to become resilient as it faces upheaval in the business environment.

As the family enterprise enters an especially volatile era, it needs to deal with unforeseen adversity.

Generative families see themselves as being on a journey, continually reinventing themselves. Each family can define specific key events marking change and development.

The first generation is when the family business is founded. The path to the second generation is called “Opportunities for the rising generation” and contains two milestones. The first is expansion and growth; good management. The second is death of a family leader.

The second generation is when a passion for new directions emerges. The path to the third generation is called “Major changes in the family enterprise” and contains two milestones. The first is plateau; diminished returns. The second is harvesting, pruning and diversifying.

The third generation is the recruiting of nonfamily directors. The path to the fourth generations includes five milestones. The first is annual family summits; family council; The second is young members rise as older members pass away. The third is education program. While every journey is different, families report four major transformations as they evolve. They occur in different ways and at different rates.

The graphic shows the four transformations that occur during the evolution of a family enterprise. In chronological order from left to right, they are harvesting, pruning, diversifying and grounding.

We see a three-phase resilience cycle in how generative families respond to change.

The graphic shows the three-phase cycle of resilience, noting how generative families respond to change. Each phase is in a circle with an arrow pointing to the next phase.

The first phase is Prepare/Anticipate and it is when the family expects and remains open to new ways.

The second phase is Engage/Decide and it is when family members listen to all points of view.

The third phase is Refine/Renew and it is when the family finds a new path and works to implement it.

No social system is immune from change, whether a family or a business. Despite this reality, many systems expect the current reality to continue and resist efforts to prepare or anticipate change. Current success can fade as new conditions arise. Capable family leaders die, move on or lose their touch, and they have to be replaced. A family enterprise should regularly perform what is called a SWOT analysis, defining and facing up to the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats that lie ahead. While experts can help with this exercise, generative families find that it is something that should be open and ideally engage a number of family members, especially young people who might see themselves in future roles. The family can set aside time to discuss their SWOT findings and consider how to anticipate and respond to them by building on their resources and making possible changes.

You are born into a family, but most people grow up and create their own destinies, even when they benefit from an inheritance and family resources. However, if there is a substantial family enterprise or trust, a family member may find that the luck of their birth turns into a life sentence when partnering with siblings and cousins, who may be very different. Generative families have some trusts and holdings that cannot be split, but they usually offer an opportunity for family members to be bought out and leave some or all family enterprises to go off on their own. There are clear procedures and rules; separation is a very consequential decision, but it is a safety valve for families that run into differences or conflict about what they will do. Some families act early and split holdings to allow family branches to separate, or they offer different assets to each sibling, encouraging and supporting different paths.
